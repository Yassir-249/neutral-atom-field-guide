0.2Pasqal in sixty seconds

Pasqal is a French quantum computing company founded in 2019 as a spin-off of the Institut d'Optique near Paris. Its machines use neutral atoms: single rubidium atoms, cooled almost to absolute zero and held in a pattern by tightly focused laser beams called optical tweezers. Each atom is a qubit. Other lasers then push the atoms into very large, "puffed-up" states called Rydberg states, in which neighbouring atoms interact strongly. That interaction is what lets the atoms compute together.

Today's Pasqal machines (the Orion family) mostly run in analog mode: instead of a list of logic gates, you choose where the atoms sit and how the laser's strength and frequency change over a few microseconds. The atoms then evolve naturally, and you measure the result as a string of 0s and 1s. You repeat this hundreds of times and study the statistics.

To program these machines you use Pasqal's open-source Python library Pulser, or higher-level libraries built on top of it (QoolQit, and ready-made solvers for graph and optimisation problems). You test programs on emulators (classical software that imitates the QPU), then submit them through Pasqal Cloud or partner clouds such as Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure Quantum, OVHcloud and Scaleway.

1.1Bits and qubits

A classical bit is always exactly 0 or 1, like a light switch. A qubit is a physical system with two distinct states that can also exist in a combination of both. On Pasqal's machines, a qubit is a single atom, and the two states are two of its energy levels: usually the ground state, written |g⟩, and a highly excited Rydberg state, written |r⟩. Pulser's own definition: when exactly two of an atom's energy levels are used to store information, that atom is a qubit. (Use three or more levels and it becomes a "qudit".)

The strange-looking brackets, |g⟩ and |r⟩, are just labels for states. Physicists call this notation a "ket". You will also see |0⟩ and |1⟩, which mean the same two states once they are used as computer bits.

1.2Superposition: amplitudes and probabilities

When both α and β are non-zero, the qubit is in a superposition. This does not mean "it is secretly 0 or 1 and we don't know which". It genuinely holds both possibilities at once, and the evidence is interference (section 1.6), which a hidden coin could never produce.

Amplitudes are richer than probabilities. Each one has a size and a phase (a direction, like the hand of a clock). Probability keeps only the size (|α|²) and throws the phase away. But the phase is what makes quantum effects possible, so the laser pulses you will write in Pulser control phase as carefully as they control probability.

Everyday picture

A spinning coin is neither heads nor tails while it spins; it carries a "recipe" for how likely each side is when it lands. The amplitude is the recipe, and the probability is what you see when it lands. The analogy breaks down in one important way: real amplitudes can cancel each other out, like waves.

1.3Measurement, shots and bitstrings

Reading a qubit forces it to choose: you get |g⟩ with probability |α|² or |r⟩ with probability |β|², and the superposition is gone. On Pasqal hardware measurement is destructive in a very literal sense. The atoms are photographed, the Rydberg atoms are lost, and that run is over.

So one run of a program gives you one bitstring: one 0 or 1 per atom, in register order. To learn the probabilities, you repeat the identical program many times. Each repetition is called a shot (Pasqal also says run). Results arrive as a count of how often each bitstring appeared, which you can draw as a histogram.

Key idea

A quantum program's output is a probability distribution, estimated from shots. More shots give a sharper estimate: the statistical error shrinks roughly like 1/√(shots). Pasqal's QPUs accept up to 500 shots per job, and each shot takes seconds, so choosing the shot count is a real budgeting decision.

1.4The Bloch sphere: a picture of one qubit

Every possible state of a single qubit can be drawn as an arrow from the centre of a sphere to a point on its surface. This is the Bloch sphere. The north pole is |r⟩ (reads as 1), the south pole is |g⟩ (reads as 0), and every point on the equator is a 50/50 superposition; the points differ only in their phase. The height of the arrow sets the probabilities. Its direction around the equator is the phase.

1.5Entanglement

Two or more qubits are entangled when their joint state cannot be described as "this atom's state" plus "that atom's state". The pair has a definite shared state even though neither atom has one of its own. Measuring one atom then tells you something about the other.

The right-hand histogram above shows this. After that pulse the two close atoms are in the state (|g r⟩ + |r g⟩)/√2: "one of us is excited, but neither of us knows which". Each atom alone looks like a fair coin, yet the two results are perfectly anti-correlated: whenever one reads 1, the other reads 0. On Pasqal machines, entanglement comes from the strong interaction between Rydberg atoms, which Part 2 explains.

Everyday picture

Two coins sealed in a box, guaranteed to land opposite. Neither coin has an answer until you open the box, but the pair always does. (Real entanglement is stronger than any such hidden arrangement, which has been proven experimentally. Alain Aspect, one of the scientists connected to Pasqal's founding, shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics for those experiments.)

1.6Interference, and why quantum computers might help

Amplitudes add up like waves before they are squared into probabilities. Two contributions with opposite phase cancel; two with the same phase reinforce. Quantum algorithms are designed so that the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel and those of right answers build up. That is interference, and it is where any quantum speed-up comes from.

The reason quantum states are hard to imitate is size. A register of N qubits needs 2N amplitudes to describe completely. Storing them on an ordinary computer takes 16 bytes each:

1.7Hamiltonians: the rulebook for how states change

You will meet the word Hamiltonian (written H) constantly in Pasqal's documentation. A Hamiltonian is the system's energy function. It lists every influence acting on the atoms (the lasers, the interactions between atoms) and assigns an energy to every possible arrangement. It is also the rule that says how the quantum state changes over time, through the Schrödinger equation:

Schrödinger equation (as written in the Pulser docs)
id|Ψ(t)⟩dt=H(t)ℏ|Ψ(t)⟩

You never solve this equation on the QPU yourself. "Evolving under a Hamiltonian" means that you set up H, by choosing where the atoms sit and what the laser does, and then let physics run. The atoms do the calculation simply by existing. An emulator does the same calculation slowly, on a classical computer.

Ground states and energy landscapes

Because H gives every arrangement an energy, you can picture an energy landscape: hills for costly arrangements, valleys for cheap ones. The lowest point is the ground state. Most of Pasqal's optimisation methods rest on one sentence: encode your problem so that its best answer is the ground state, then guide the atoms into it.

Everyday picture

Pour water onto a crumpled sheet of paper. You don't compute where it will go; it finds the dips by itself. Building the right Hamiltonian is crumpling the paper so that the deepest dip is your answer.

Check yourself · Part 1tap a question to reveal the answer
A qubit has α = β = 1/√2. What are the chances of reading 0 and 1?

|α|² = |β|² = 1/2, so 50% each. That state sits on the equator of the Bloch sphere.

Why can't you learn a qubit's amplitudes from a single shot?

A shot returns one bit and destroys the superposition. You estimate probabilities by repeating the program many times and counting.

In the right-hand histogram, each atom alone reads 1 about half the time. Why is the pair entangled rather than two independent coins?

Independent coins would sometimes both read 1 (and sometimes both 0). Here 11 and 00 never happen: the results are perfectly anti-correlated, which a product of two separate states cannot produce.

In one sentence, what is a Hamiltonian?

The energy function of the system, which also dictates how its quantum state evolves in time.

2.1Why build a computer out of atoms?

Pasqal's machines use single atoms of rubidium-87, a soft metal whose atoms are easy to cool and trap with standard lasers and can be excited to very high energy levels. The atoms are neutral (they carry no electric charge), which is what distinguishes this approach from trapped-ion computers. Pasqal gives three main reasons for the choice:

Identical by nature

Every rubidium atom is a perfect copy of every other. There is no manufacturing variation to calibrate away, unlike qubits printed on a chip.

No giant fridge

The atoms are cooled to millionths of a degree above absolute zero, but only the atoms. The vacuum chamber and the room stay at room temperature; an Orion Beta system draws about 10 kW.

Any shape you like

Lasers place atoms in almost any pattern in 1D or 2D (3D in research). Changing the layout is a software change, not a new chip.

2.2Inside the machine

Magneto-optical trap (MOT)

Laser beams and a magnetic field slow a cloud of about a million atoms (roughly 1 mm³) until it is a few millionths of a degree above absolute zero. Temperature is motion, so slowing atoms cools them.

Optical tweezers

Tightly focused laser spots about 1 µm wide. An atom is pulled into the brightest point of a spot and held there. One tweezer holds at most one atom, so one tweezer means one qubit.

SLM (spatial light modulator)

A programmable hologram that splits one laser into hundreds of fixed tweezer spots in any pattern. It creates the trap layout.

AOD (acousto-optic deflector)

A crystal driven by sound waves that steers a laser beam. It makes a movable tweezer, used to carry atoms around when sorting them.

Rydberg excitation lasers

No single laser can lift rubidium from the ground state to a Rydberg level, so Pasqal's Fresnel machines use two photons: 420 nm (violet) then 1013 nm (infrared), tuned to act as one transition.

Camera

Atoms lit by resonant light glow (fluoresce). A sensitive camera records which trap sites are bright, which is how atoms are counted and results are read.

2.3Loading and sorting atoms

Filling the tweezers is a game of chance: each one captures an atom only about 50% of the time. A fresh array therefore comes up roughly half full, in a different random pattern on every shot. To get the exact register you asked for, the machine photographs the array, then a moving tweezer carries atoms from spare traps into the empty sites of your pattern. This rearrangement succeeds more than 99% of the time, and each move takes under a millisecond.

2.5Driving atoms with light: Ω, δ and φ

A laser pulse acting on the |g⟩ ↔ |r⟩ transition is described by exactly three numbers that can change over time. These are the only knobs you have, and every Pulser Pulse sets all three.

Amplitude Ω (Rabi frequency)

How strongly the laser drives the atom, which sets how fast it swings between |g⟩ and |r⟩. In rad/µs. Pasqal's QPUs allow up to 2 MHz, which is 12.57 rad/µs.

Detuning δ

How far the laser frequency is from the atom's exact transition frequency. δ = 0 is "on resonance". In energy terms, δ is the reward (positive) or cost (negative) of being excited.

Phase φ

The laser's starting phase. It does not change the speed, only the axis of rotation on the Bloch sphere. Constant within a single pulse.

With the laser on, the atom oscillates back and forth between |g⟩ and |r⟩. These are Rabi oscillations. For a constant pulse, the probability of finding the atom in |r⟩ after time t is:

Global and local addressing

A global laser illuminates the whole array, so every atom receives the same Ω, δ and φ. A local beam targets chosen atoms. Pasqal's current cloud QPUs offer only a global Rydberg laser. That sounds limiting, but it is the normal mode for analog computing: because every atom gets the same pulse, the individual behaviour comes from where the atoms are placed, through the interaction described next.

Everyday picture

A global laser is one loudspeaker for the whole room; a local laser whispers to one person. With only the loudspeaker, your "program" is largely the seating plan.

2.6Rydberg atoms and the blockade

In a Rydberg state, the atom's outermost electron is pushed into a very high orbit (principal quantum number n = 60 on Pasqal's QPUs). The orbit's radius grows like n², so the atom becomes thousands of times larger than normal and extremely sensitive to its neighbours. Two Rydberg atoms a distance R apart repel each other with an energy that falls off very steeply:

Van der Waals interaction between two Rydberg atoms
U(R)=C6R6
C₆a constant set by the Rydberg level; 865,723 rad·µm⁶/µs for n = 60Rdistance between the atoms, in µm1/R⁶double the distance and the interaction drops 64×

Now the key effect. Suppose two atoms sit close together and a laser tries to excite both. Exciting the first is fine. But exciting the second as well would cost the extra energy U(R), which shifts the "both excited" state |rr⟩ so far off resonance that the laser simply cannot reach it. The pair can hold at most one excitation. This is the Rydberg blockade, first demonstrated between two individual atoms in 2009 by Antoine Browaeys's group, whose members later co-founded Pasqal.

The blockade switches on when the interaction beats the laser drive: C₆/R⁶ greater than Ω. Setting the two equal defines the blockade radius:

Atoms closer than Rb block each other; atoms further apart act almost independently. It is a soft crossover, not a hard wall, and the approximation is least accurate when R is close to Rb. Notice two practical facts. The blockade radius at full power (about 6.4 µm) is just above the QPU's 5 µm minimum spacing, so nearest neighbours block and next-nearest usually don't. And you can change Rb without moving any atom, simply by changing Ω.

1 · Drive: the mixer

Ω flips atoms back and forth and creates superpositions, letting the system explore many arrangements at once.

2 · Detuning: the reward

−δ·n: each excited atom changes the energy by −δ. Positive δ makes excitations cheap ("get as many 1s as you can"); negative δ makes them costly.

3 · Interaction: the penalty

C₆/R⁶ for every pair that is both excited. Always positive, always on, and set purely by where the atoms are.

Everyday picture · seating a wedding

The drive is guests milling around. The detuning is "you score a point for every guest who sits down". The interaction is "these two guests must not both sit; they're feuding". You want the most points without seating a feuding pair, and you describe the feuds purely by where you put the chairs. That is exactly how Pasqal encodes optimisation problems.

Watch out · sign conventions

The minus sign in front of sin φ comes from Pulser's internal ordering of states (|r⟩ listed first). If you relabel |g⟩ as 0 and |r⟩ as 1 in the usual textbook way, that sign becomes plus. Pulser's Conventions page spells out both. Also note that the interaction term never turns off, even during a delay with the laser off: atoms always "feel" each other whenever more than one is excited.

Two variants also appear in the docs. In XY mode (two Rydberg levels driven by microwaves), the interaction lets atoms swap an excitation, with strength C₃/R³ that reaches further than C₆/R⁶. With a detuning map (DMM), an extra term −Σ εₖ δDMM(t) nₖ gives each atom its own weight εₖ between 0 and 1.

2.8Reading out the answer

At the end of a sequence the machine takes another fluorescence image. Here is the clever part: the tweezers hold ground-state atoms but repel Rydberg atoms, which therefore escape. So in the final image a bright site means the atom was in |g⟩ (read as 0), and a dark site means it was in |r⟩ and left (read as 1). Detection efficiency in this kind of setup exceeds 98.6%.

Everyday picture

A row of candles. Your pulse blows across them; the candles that went out are the atoms you excited. You read the answer off which ones are dark.

Because the measurement removes atoms, you get one bitstring per shot, you cannot peek at the state mid-sequence, and the whole register must be rebuilt for the next shot.

2.9One shot, from start to finish

Different sources quote different repetition rates because they measure different things. A research setup's raw cycle is about 200 ms (around 5 Hz). Pasqal's Orion Beta specification gives a minimum effective rate of 0.25 Hz, counting only successful computations, and notes that it depends on the number of qubits. Pasqal Cloud uses the same 4 seconds per shot as a deliberately cautious cost estimate.

Why can a register use at most 45% of a layout's traps?

Tweezers load randomly, about half the time each. Spare traps guarantee enough atoms are available to sort into your requested pattern.

3.1Analog, digital and digital-analog

There are two broad ways to program a quantum computer, and neutral atoms can do both.

Analog

No gates. You place atoms and shape Ω(t) and δ(t), and the whole register evolves under one Hamiltonian. Pasqal argues this suits today's noisy machines: one smooth analog step can replace a very long chain of error-prone gates.

Digital

Single-qubit gates come from Raman lasers on the hyperfine qubit (|g⟩, |h⟩). Two-qubit gates use the blockade. Because many atoms can share one blockade region, multi-qubit gates such as Toffoli can be native.

Digital-analog

A hybrid: digital gates on the hyperfine levels, combined with blocks of analog evolution that use the Rydberg level for entanglement. This was the focus of Pasqal's Qadence library (Part 7).

How the blockade makes a two-qubit gate

The classic controlled-Z (CZ) gate uses three pulses: a π-pulse on the control atom, a 2π-pulse on the target, then another π-pulse on the control. A 2π round trip from |g⟩ to |r⟩ and back leaves the target with a minus sign. But if the control was excited, the blockade stops the target's round trip, so no minus sign appears. That conditional minus sign is exactly a CZ gate. In research setups such gates take about a microsecond.

Everyday picture

The target tries to go out for a walk and come back, and gets a stamp on its passport when it does. If the control is standing in the doorway, the target can't leave, and the stamp is missing. The missing stamp is the gate.

3.2Quantum simulation: atoms imitating materials

The Hamiltonian in Part 2 is, almost exactly, a textbook model of magnetism (the transverse-field Ising model). Each atom acts like a tiny magnet that can point up (excited) or down (ground). So the most direct use of a neutral-atom QPU is to simulate magnetic materials by building them atom by atom and watching what happens. This is the oldest and best-established use of the technology. Pasqal's own blog points to a 2021 Nature experiment by the Browaeys and Lahaye group with 196 atoms, and a 2026 preprint reports a one-to-one simulation of a frustrated magnet with 256 qubits.

A key concept here is the quantum phase transition. As you change δ/Ω, the lowest-energy arrangement of the atoms can suddenly reorganise: from "all in |g⟩" to a checkerboard where every second atom is excited (called Z₂ order), or, if the blockade covers two neighbours, to "every third atom excited" (Z₃ order). These transitions are driven by quantum effects, not by temperature, and they are exactly where classical simulation struggles most.

Everyday picture

Seat people with the rule "no two neighbours" and you get every other seat. Change the rule to "no one within two seats" and the pattern suddenly jumps to every third seat. The point where the pattern flips is the phase transition.

3.3The quantum adiabatic algorithm

The adiabatic theorem says that if a system starts in its lowest-energy state and you change its Hamiltonian slowly enough, it stays in the lowest-energy state of the Hamiltonian at each moment. That gives a general recipe for optimisation, the Quantum Adiabatic Algorithm (QAA):

  1. Start easy. With strongly negative detuning, excitations are expensive, so the lowest-energy state is simply "every atom in |g⟩". That is exactly how the atoms start.
  2. Sweep slowly. Turn the laser on, then sweep δ from negative, through zero, to positive. Excitations become rewarded, but the blockade still forbids neighbours both being excited.
  3. End at the problem. Turn the laser off at the end. Now the lowest-energy state is the best arrangement of excitations allowed by your geometry: the answer.
  4. Measure many times. The most frequent bitstrings are the best candidates.
Everyday picture

Carrying a full bowl of soup across a room. Walk slowly and the surface stays flat; rush and it sloshes over. The narrowest doorway, where you must slow down most, is the minimum gap.

The antiferromagnet program in Part 5 is a QAA. Pulser's QUBO tutorial uses the same shape with δ swept from −5 to +5 rad/µs over 4 µs, and shows the average cost improving as the sweep gets longer.

3.4Maximum independent set: the native problem

Draw a graph: dots (nodes) joined by lines (edges). An independent set is a group of nodes with no edge between any two of them. The maximum independent set (MIS) is the largest such group. Finding it is famously hard for large graphs (NP-hard), and it appears everywhere: scheduling jobs that can't overlap, placing antennas that mustn't interfere, choosing non-conflicting satellite observations.

Now take a unit-disk graph: nodes are points on a plane, and two nodes are joined exactly when they are closer than some distance. Put one atom on each node and set the laser so the blockade radius equals that distance. Physics now enforces the rule for you: two connected atoms cannot both be excited. Every pattern of excitations the atoms can reach is an independent set, and the detuning rewards having as many excitations as possible. So the ground state at the end of an adiabatic sweep is the maximum independent set. There is no encoding overhead: the hardware's own constraint is the problem's constraint.

Weighted problems (MWIS)

In the maximum weighted independent set problem, each node has a value and you maximise the total value rather than the count. Pasqal handles the weights with a detuning map (DMM): each atom gets an extra detuning according to its weight, so heavier nodes are more strongly rewarded for being excited. 3.5QUBO: binary optimisation in matrix form

Many business problems (portfolios, routes, schedules) can be written as a QUBO, a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation. You have yes/no decisions z₁…zₙ (each 0 or 1) and a symmetric table of numbers Q. The goal is to choose the decisions that make this cost as small as possible:

There are real limits, though. Interactions are always positive, so only QUBOs with positive off-diagonal entries embed directly. And because interaction strength is a fixed function of distance in 2D, an arbitrary matrix usually can't be reproduced exactly. Finding good positions is itself an optimisation problem; Pasqal's QUBO solver ships dedicated embedding methods (Greedy Layout, BLaDE) for it.

Everyday picture

You have a table of how much each pair of guests dislike each other, and only one tool: distance. Closer means more friction. Embedding a QUBO is arranging the chairs so the distances reproduce the dislike table as closely as possible, and sometimes no arrangement fits perfectly.

3.6Variational loops: a classical computer in charge

Often you don't know the best pulse shape in advance. In a variational (hybrid quantum-classical) algorithm, a classical optimiser proposes pulse parameters, the QPU runs them and returns bitstrings, a cost function scores the result, and the optimiser proposes better parameters. QAOA (the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm) is the best-known example: it alternates between "problem" and "mixing" evolutions whose durations are tuned in the loop.

Pulser's tutorial on Bayesian optimisation tunes the antiferromagnet schedule this way, adjusting Ω and δ at a few interpolation points to maximise a score of how checkerboard-like the result is.

3.7Quantum machine learning on graphs

Graphs are a natural data type for neutral atoms, because a register is a graph. Pasqal's flagship machine-learning method, the Quantum Evolution Kernel (QEK), uses that directly:

  1. Embed. Turn each graph (for example a molecule: atoms as nodes, bonds as edges) into a register, with edges becoming blockade pairs.
  2. Evolve. Apply the same fixed pulse to every graph's register.
  3. Sample. Measure many shots. The bitstring distribution is a "fingerprint" of the graph's structure.
  4. Compare. The similarity of two graphs is computed from how different their fingerprints are.
  5. Learn. Feed the similarity table to an ordinary classical classifier (a support vector machine).

Everyday picture

Play the same piece of music in differently shaped concert halls. Each hall's echo is unique. You can compare two halls by comparing their echoes, without ever seeing a blueprint.

3.8Simulating atoms on classical computers

Emulators are central to the platform: you develop and test on them, and you compare QPU results against them. How far they can go depends on how they store the quantum state.

State vector (exact)

Store all 2N amplitudes. Exact for any entanglement and geometry, but every extra atom doubles the memory and time. Practical up to about 25 atoms (EMU-SV), less with noise.

Matrix product state (approximate)

Store a chain of small tensors, one per atom, linked by "bonds". The bond dimension measures how much entanglement the chain can hold. Low entanglement: small bonds, cheap. High entanglement: bonds explode. Up to 80 atoms on EMU-MPS.

The catch is that entanglement peaks exactly at the quantum phase transitions that adiabatic algorithms must pass through. Two-dimensional registers make it worse, because a 2D grid must be "snaked" into a 1D chain, turning short-range neighbours into long-range chain links. So classical emulation gives out precisely in the regime where the QPU is most interesting. That boundary is where neutral-atom hardware has to earn its keep.

Everyday picture

Describing a crowd. A state vector lists every possible arrangement of everyone: complete but astronomically long. An MPS records each person plus how tightly they hold hands with the next person. For a queue, that's short and accurate. For a mosh pit, there's no shortcut.

Check yourself · Part 3tap a question to reveal the answer
Why does the adiabatic sweep start with negative detuning?

Negative δ makes excitations expensive, so the lowest-energy state is "all atoms in |g⟩", which is exactly the state the atoms start in.

Why is MIS on unit-disk graphs "native" to neutral atoms?

The blockade automatically forbids two connected atoms from both being excited, which is exactly the independence constraint. No extra encoding is needed.

Which QUBO matrices can be embedded directly, and why?

Those with positive off-diagonal entries, because the van der Waals interaction is always repulsive (positive). Even then, a 2D geometry may not reproduce every value exactly.

What does the bond dimension of an MPS measure?

How much entanglement the representation can hold across each cut of the chain. More entanglement needs a larger bond dimension and more compute.

5.2The mental model: five objects

Every Pulser program, however large, is built from the same five objects. Learn their roles once and the rest is detail.

Register

A named set of atom positions in micrometres. The order you list atoms in fixes the order of bits in your results.

Device

A description of a machine: how many atoms, how close they may be, which lasers exist and how strong they can be. Pulser ships example devices; real ones are downloaded from the cloud.

Your program. You declare channels (lasers available on the device) and add pulses to them, one after another.

Something that runs a sequence: QutipBackendV2 on your laptop, RemoteMPSBackend in the cloud, QPUBackend on real atoms.

Counts of measured bitstrings, plus any observables you asked an emulator to compute (occupations, energy, fidelity…).

Key idea · the mixed units work out

Durations are in nanoseconds, but frequencies are per microsecond. A pulse of 1000 ns at Ω = π rad/µs has area π × 1 µs = π: exactly a π-pulse. Hardware datasheets often quote frequencies in MHz; multiply by 2π to get rad/µs. The example device's maximum amplitude, 12.566 rad/µs, is 2 MHz × 2π.

Every atom starts in its ground state. In the standard ground-rydberg basis, an atom measured in the Rydberg state reads as 1, and an atom in the ground state reads as 0. Bits appear in the same order as the atoms in your register, so for atoms q0, q1, q2 the bitstring "010" means only q1 was excited.

5.4Registers and layouts

A register is a dictionary of atom names and positions. Pulser offers shortcuts for common shapes.

python
import numpy as np
import pulser
from pulser import Register

reg = Register({"q0": (0, 0), "q1": (5, 0), "q2": (2.5, 4.33)})   # by hand, in µm
sq  = Register.square(3, spacing=6.0, prefix="q")                 # 3x3 grid: q0 ... q8
tri = Register.triangular_lattice(rows=3, atoms_per_row=4, spacing=5.0, prefix="q")
hexr = Register.hexagon(layers=2, spacing=5.0, prefix="q")        # rings around a centre atom
pts = Register.from_coordinates([(0, 0), (7, 0), (14, 0)], prefix="q")

# Draw it, with lines between atoms that sit inside each other's blockade radius
R_b = pulser.AnalogDevice.rydberg_blockade_radius(2 * np.pi)      # µm, for Ω = 2π rad/µs
sq.draw(blockade_radius=R_b, draw_graph=True, draw_half_radius=True)

With draw_half_radius=True, each atom gets a circle of radius Rb/2; where two circles overlap, those atoms block each other. This picture is the fastest way to check that your register encodes the graph you intended.

Layouts: why real hardware needs them

On the real machine, atoms can only sit where the optical system has made a trap. A register layout is the full set of trap positions. Your register then picks which traps to fill. Calibrating a new layout takes the Pasqal team time, so it is best to reuse pre-calibrated layouts.

python
from pulser.register.special_layouts import TriangularLatticeLayout

device = pulser.AnalogDevice
print(device.requires_layout)            # True: a bare Register will not pass QPU checks
print(device.calibrated_register_layouts.keys())   # ['TriangularLatticeLayout(61, 5.0µm)']

# Option 1: let Pulser build a compliant layout around your register
reg_ok = sq.with_automatic_layout(device)

# Option 2: pick traps from a layout yourself
layout = TriangularLatticeLayout(n_traps=61, spacing=5)
reg_from_layout = layout.define_register(26, 35, 36, 27, qubit_ids=["a", "b", "c", "d"])

# Option 3: decide which traps to use later, at build time
mappable = layout.make_mappable_register(n_qubits=4)   # ids q0..q3
Watch out

Pulser validates distances immediately. Putting atoms 2 µm apart on AnalogDevice raises ValueError: The minimal distance between atoms in this device (5 µm) is not respected. Read the error; it tells you which rule you broke.

Modulation bandwidth

Real lasers can't change instantly. Pulser models this with mod_bandwidth (8 MHz here): your programmed "input" is smoothed into an "output". seq.draw() shows both. Very short square pulses may never reach their programmed height.

EOM mode

A special mode that gives sharper square pulses (40 MHz bandwidth on AnalogDevice), entered with enable_eom_mode. Useful when you need crisp on/off control.

DMM and detuning maps

Assign each atom a weight between 0 and 1, then apply a negative detuning scaled by those weights. This is how weighted problems (MWIS) put different "rewards" on different atoms.

SLM mask

Detunes chosen atoms far off resonance so a global pulse skips them. The standard trick for preparing an uneven starting state.

5.7Pulses and waveforms

A Pulse bundles three things over one duration: an amplitude waveform Ω(t), a detuning waveform δ(t), and a fixed phase φ. Both waveforms must have the same duration.

Key idea · pulse area

The area of an amplitude waveform is ∫Ω dt, in radians. For one atom on resonance, area π flips |g⟩ to |r⟩ (a π-pulse), area π/2 makes an equal superposition, and area 2π brings it back. BlackmanWaveform(1000, np.pi) has area exactly π, with a peak of about 7.49 rad/µs. Blackman shapes are preferred to square pulses because their smooth edges avoid exciting unwanted frequencies.

5.8Building a sequence

A sequence ties a register to a device. You declare the channels you will use, then add pulses to them in order.

python
from pulser import Sequence
from pulser.devices import MockDevice

seq = Sequence(reg, MockDevice)                 # MockDevice: unconstrained, good for learning
print(seq.available_channels.keys())            # what this device offers

seq.declare_channel("ryd", "rydberg_global")    # your name, the device's channel id
seq.add(p2, "ryd")                              # pulses run one after another
seq.delay(200, "ryd")                           # wait 200 ns
seq.add(p1, "ryd")

seq.measure("ground-rydberg")                   # optional: measurement basis
print(seq.get_duration(), "ns")
print(seq)                                      # text schedule
seq.draw()                                      # plot: amplitude, detuning, phase

When a sequence uses several channels (for example a global and a local laser), add takes a protocol argument that decides how a new pulse lines up with the others:

"min-delay" (default)

Wait only as long as needed to avoid clashing with other channels acting on the same atoms.

"wait-for-all"

Wait until every channel is free, then start.

"no-delay"

Start immediately, even if that overlaps other pulses.

Other useful methods: align(*channels) lines channels up in time; target(qubits, channel) moves a local channel's aim; phase_shift(phi, *qubits) applies a "virtual Z" rotation for free; with_new_device(device) re-checks the whole sequence against another device; to_abstract_repr() saves it as JSON, which is how sequences travel to the cloud.

All 1000 simulated shots read 1: a perfect π-pulse. Now experiment. Change the duration to 500 ns and you should see roughly half 0 and half 1. Change it to 2000 ns and you should be back to all 0. You are tracing a Rabi oscillation, the same curve as in the lab in Part 2.

5.10A real analog program: an antiferromagnet on a 3×3 grid

This is Pulser's classic tutorial and a template for many analog algorithms. Nine atoms sit on a square grid. We slowly sweep the laser detuning from negative to positive, and the atoms settle into a checkerboard pattern: excited, not excited, excited… This is called an antiferromagnetic (AFM) state, and it is the lowest-energy arrangement when neighbours block each other.

python · tested on Pulser 1.9.1
import numpy as np
import pulser

U = 2 * np.pi                    # target neighbour interaction, rad/µs
Omega_max = 2.0 * U              # 12.57 rad/µs: the device maximum
delta_0, delta_f = -6 * U, 2 * U
t_rise, t_fall = 252, 500
t_sweep = int((delta_f - delta_0) / (2 * np.pi * 10) * 1000)   # 800 ns

device = pulser.AnalogDevice
R = (device.interaction_coeff / U) ** (1 / 6)    # spacing so that C6/R^6 = U  -> 7.19 µm
reg = pulser.Register.square(3, R, prefix="q")

seq = pulser.Sequence(reg, device)
seq.declare_channel("rydberg_global", "rydberg_global")
seq.add(pulser.Pulse.ConstantDetuning(
    pulser.RampWaveform(t_rise, 0.0, Omega_max), delta_0, 0.0), "rydberg_global")
seq.add(pulser.Pulse.ConstantAmplitude(
    Omega_max, pulser.RampWaveform(t_sweep, delta_0, delta_f), 0.0), "rydberg_global")
seq.add(pulser.Pulse.ConstantDetuning(
    pulser.RampWaveform(t_fall, Omega_max, 0.0), delta_f, 0.0), "rydberg_global")

print(seq.get_duration(), "ns")          # 1552 ns, well inside the 6000 ns limit

results = pulser.backends.QutipBackendV2(seq).run()
counts = results.final_bitstrings
print(sorted(counts.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:3])
output
1552 ns
[('101010101', 105), ('101000101', 45), ('111000101', 22)]

The most common outcome, 101010101, is the checkerboard: atoms 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 (the corners and the centre) excited, the edge atoms not. Other outcomes appear because the sweep is finite and the edges of a small grid are imperfect. That is normal: on a QPU you always study a distribution, never a single answer.

Key idea · the recipe behind most analog algorithms

Choose atom positions so that the interactions encode your problem. Start the laser far below resonance, where "all atoms in |g⟩" is the lowest-energy state. Sweep slowly to a point where the lowest-energy state is the answer you want. Measure many times. The MIS and QUBO solvers in Part 7 automate exactly these steps.

5.11Parametrized sequences

When you want to try many variants of one program (different amplitudes, durations or atom choices), declare variables instead of hard-coding numbers. You build the concrete sequence later. This is also how one cloud batch can hold many related jobs.

python
seq = pulser.Sequence(reg, pulser.AnalogDevice)
seq.declare_channel("ryd", "rydberg_global")

omega = seq.declare_variable("omega")                 # a float
t = seq.declare_variable("t", dtype=int)              # an integer duration in ns
seq.add(pulser.Pulse.ConstantPulse(t, omega, 0.0, 0.0), "ryd")

print(seq.is_parametrized())                          # True: can't be run or drawn yet
built = seq.build(omega=np.pi, t=1000)                # now it is a normal sequence
built.draw()

Variables support arithmetic (delta_0 = -6 * omega), so whole schedules can hang off one or two numbers. A MappableRegister works the same way for atom positions: you choose the traps at build(qubits={...}) time.

Check yourself · Part 5tap a question to reveal the answer
A pulse has duration 500 and amplitude π. What fraction of the way from |g⟩ to |r⟩ does it take one atom?

Area = π rad/µs × 0.5 µs = π/2. That is half a π-pulse, so the atom ends in an equal superposition: about 50% of shots read 1.

Your register has atoms a, b, c (in that order). What does the bitstring "001" mean?

Only atom c was measured in the Rydberg state; a and b were in the ground state.

Why does a hand-made Register fail when you target a real QPU?

Real devices require a register layout (requires_layout=True): atoms must sit in calibrated trap positions. Use with_automatic_layout(device) or build the register from a pre-calibrated layout.

In the AFM program, why is the spacing set to (C₆/U)^(1/6)?

Because the interaction between two atoms is C₆/R⁶. Choosing R that way makes the neighbour interaction exactly U, the energy scale the detuning sweep was designed around.

What does evaluation time 0.5 mean for an observable?

The halfway point of the whole sequence. Evaluation times are fractions of the total duration, not nanoseconds.

6.11Checklist before your first QPU job

  1. Fetch the live device with connection.fetch_available_devices() and build the sequence on it.
  2. Use a calibrated layout. Prefer device.pre_calibrated_layouts; a brand-new layout needs fresh calibration, which takes time.
  3. Stay inside the limits: durations in multiples of the clock period, total under 6 µs, amplitude and detuning within the channel maxima.
  4. Emulate with noise (prefer_device_noise_model=True) so you know what "good" looks like on noisy hardware.
  5. Dry-run with mimic_qpu=True on an emulator backend.
  6. Choose shots deliberately. At most 500 per job on Orion devices; more shots shrink statistical error, but cost more.
  7. Record the batch ID and compare QPU counts with your emulated baseline.

7.1QoolQit: analog programs without units

QoolQit is a Python library for designing analog programs in what Pasqal calls the Rydberg Analog Model. Its central idea is adimensionalisation: you write programs without hardware units (no µm, ns or rad/µs), which makes them independent of any particular device. A separate compilation step then converts your unit-free program into physical numbers that fit a chosen device.

That separation is useful. The problem you are solving (a graph, a pattern of weights) stays clean, and the details of one machine's spacing or laser limits are handled by the compiler. QoolQit is actively maintained and is the recommended starting point for new analog algorithm work (current docs version: 1.4.0).

python · from the QoolQit docs
# pip install qoolqit
from qoolqit import ConstantWaveform, Drive, InterpolatedWaveform, RampWaveform

# Waveforms take a duration first (unit-free), and chain with >>
amplitude = ConstantWaveform(5.0, value=1.0) >> InterpolatedWaveform(10.0, [0.0, 0.8, 0.8, 0.0])
detuning  = RampWaveform(8.0, -1.0, 1.0) >> ConstantWaveform(4.0, 1.0)

drive = Drive(amplitude=amplitude, detuning=detuning)   # omitted detuning/phase default to zero
drive = drive >> drive                                  # drives compose too
drive.draw()
Key objects

Register and DataGraph (where atoms go, and the graph they represent), waveforms, Drive (amplitude + detuning + phase over time), QuantumProgram(register, drive), devices, and embedders that turn data into registers.

Workflow

Build a register and a drive, combine them into a QuantumProgram, compile it for a target device (which produces a Pulser sequence), then execute it on an emulator or QPU.

Its docs are organised as Get started, Fundamentals (graphs, embedding, writing, compiling and executing programs), Extended usage (adimensionalisation, custom embedders) and Applications (solving a QUBO, solving a MWIS problem, preparing the critical state of the transverse-field Ising model). At the time of writing, Pasqal is also running a QoolQit Contest on its community site, with QPU credits as prizes.

Watch out

7.2The MIS solver

The maximum-independent-set library compiles MIS problems into sequences for analog hardware. You don't need any quantum knowledge to try it: hand it a graph and get back optimal or near-optimal independent sets. Advanced users can tune the strategy, run on Pasqal Cloud, or build custom pipelines.

python · quickstart from the docs
# pip install maximum-independent-set
from mis import MISSolver, MISInstance, BackendConfig, SolverConfig
import networkx as nx

graph = nx.Graph()
graph.add_edges_from([(0, 1), (0, 2)])     # a path of three nodes: 1 - 0 - 2
instance = MISInstance(graph)

config = SolverConfig(backend=BackendConfig())   # default: a local quantum emulator
solver = MISSolver(instance, config)
results = solver.solve()

print("MIS solutions:", results)              # the best answer is nodes 1 and 2 (the two ends)
results[0].draw()

The tutorials cover weighted MIS, programming the quantum part yourself, working around device constraints, and solving MIS through a QUBO formulation. Graph colouring lives in the same package (mis.coloring).

7.3The QUBO solver

The qubo-solver library takes a symmetric matrix Q and searches for the binary vector that minimises the QUBO cost from Part 3. It offers quantum, classical and hybrid solvers, so you can compare them on the same instance.

python · quickstart from the docs
# pip install qubo-solver
from qubosolver import Instance, Solver, matrix, analysis

Q = matrix.tensor([
    [-0.2, 0.0, 1.0],
    [ 0.0, -1.0, 1.5],
    [ 1.0, 1.5, -0.1],
])
instance = Instance(Q)

solver = Solver(instance)        # the quantum solver is the default
solution = solver.solve()
print(analysis.to_dataframe([solution]))

How the matrix becomes atom positions: Greedy Layout and BLaDE.

How the pulse is designed: Proportional-Diagonal, Local-Energy-Scale, Bayesian Search, or your own.

7.4QEK: the quantum evolution kernel library

quantum-evolution-kernel implements the graph-similarity method from Part 3 for the machine-learning community. A typical workflow: load a molecule dataset (the tutorials use PTC-FM, a toxicity benchmark, via PyTorch Geometric), compile each graph into a register with a compiler class, run the extractor on an emulator or QPU to get bitstring distributions, then build a QuantumEvolutionKernel(mu=...) and train a scikit-learn SVC on it.

The method comes from the paper Quantum feature maps for graph machine learning on a neutral atom quantum processor (Physical Review A 107, 042615; arXiv 2107.03247). Install with pip install quantum-evolution-kernel; the tutorials walk from feature extraction to a trained classifier.

Physical vs logical qubits

A physical qubit is one atom. A logical qubit is one reliable qubit made from many physical ones using quantum error correction. Error-correction codes detect and fix errors without reading the stored information directly.

Fault tolerance

If physical error rates fall below a threshold, adding more physical qubits per logical qubit makes logical errors arbitrarily small. "Fault-tolerant" means the whole machine, correction included, works below that threshold.

9.2Cheat sheet

Units in Pulser

Time in ns (integers). Ω and δ in rad/µs. Distances in µm. Phase in rad. MHz × 2π = rad/µs. 1000 ns at Ω = π rad/µs is a π-pulse.

Bits

Atoms start in |g⟩. ground-rydberg basis: |r⟩ reads 1, |g⟩ reads 0. Bit order = register order.

Core formulas

Rabi: Pr = Ω²/(Ω²+δ²) · sin²(√(Ω²+δ²)·t/2). Pulse area θ = ∫Ω dt. Interaction U = C₆/R⁶. Blockade radius Rb = (C₆/Ω)1/6. N blockaded atoms oscillate at √N·Ω.

Hamiltonian

H = Σ (Ω/2)(cos φ σˣ − sin φ σʸ) − Σ δ nᵢ + Σ C₆/Rᵢⱼ⁶ nᵢnⱼ. Drive = mixer, detuning = reward, interaction = penalty.

Orion QPU limits (docs)

100 (Beta) / 200 (Gamma) atoms · min spacing 5 µm · max radius 35 / 70 µm · Rydberg n = 60 · C₆ = 865,723 rad·µm⁶/µs · sequence ≤ 6,000 ns · ≤ 500 shots/job · layout ≤ 200 traps, ≤ 45% filled · global Rydberg channel only.

Channel rules (AnalogDevice)

Ω ≤ 12.57 rad/µs · |δ| ≤ 125.66 rad/µs · durations multiple of 4 ns, ≥ 16 ns · Rb at max Ω ≈ 6.4 µm.

Emulator sizes

EMU-FREE ~10 atoms (free) · EMU-SV ~25 (exact) · EMU-MPS up to 80 (approximate) · QPU beyond that.

Cloud words

Run = shot = one bitstring. Job = runs with fixed variables. Batch = ≤1,000 jobs, one backend. Project = team space with an ID that pays.

import numpy as np, pulser

9.3Glossary

Pasqal's documentation has no single glossary page, so here is one. Search, or filter by topic.

Qubit Quantum

A two-level quantum system; on Pasqal machines, one atom using two of its energy levels.

Superposition Quantum

A qubit state that combines both levels at once, described by two amplitudes.

Amplitude Quantum

A complex number (size and phase) for each possibility; its squared size is the probability.

Phase Quantum

The "direction" part of an amplitude, invisible in one measurement but essential for interference. Also the laser's φ.

Measurement Quantum

Reading qubits; it returns one outcome and destroys the superposition. Destructive on neutral-atom QPUs.

Bitstring Quantum

One measured 0/1 per atom, in register order; the output of a single shot.

Bloch sphere Quantum

A sphere on which every single-qubit state is a point; pulses rotate the state around it.

Entanglement Quantum

A shared state of several qubits that can't be split into separate states for each.

Interference Quantum

Amplitudes adding or cancelling like waves; the source of quantum speed-ups.

Hamiltonian Quantum

The energy function of a system, which also dictates how its state evolves in time.

Ground state Quantum

The lowest-energy state of a Hamiltonian; in optimisation, the encoded answer. Also the atom's lowest level |g⟩.

Schrödinger equation Quantum

The equation that tells you how a quantum state changes under a Hamiltonian.

Quantum phase transition Quantum

A sudden change in a system's ground-state pattern as a parameter is tuned, driven by quantum effects.

Logical qubit Quantum

One error-protected qubit built from many physical qubits using error correction.

Fault tolerance (FTQC) Quantum

Computing reliably despite errors, by keeping physical error rates below an error-correction threshold.

NISQ Quantum

Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum: today's era of useful but error-prone machines with tens to hundreds of qubits.

Quantum advantage Quantum

Beating the best classical method on a specific, well-defined problem. Always problem-specific.

Neutral atom Hardware

An uncharged atom (rubidium-87 at Pasqal) used as a qubit.

Optical tweezer Hardware

A tightly focused laser spot (~1 µm) that traps a single atom.

SLM Hardware

Spatial light modulator: a programmable hologram that creates a pattern of fixed tweezers.

AOD Hardware

Acousto-optic deflector: steers a moving tweezer used to rearrange atoms.

MOT Hardware

Magneto-optical trap: lasers plus a magnetic field that cool a cloud of atoms to µK.

Rearrangement Hardware

Moving atoms from randomly loaded traps into the requested register; succeeds >99%.

Rydberg state Hardware

A highly excited atomic state (n = 60 on Pasqal QPUs) with very strong interactions.

Hyperfine qubit Hardware

Two closely spaced ground levels (|g⟩, |h⟩): long-lived, used for digital gates via Raman lasers.

Rabi frequency (Ω) Hardware

The laser drive strength, setting how fast an atom oscillates between levels. In rad/µs.

Detuning (δ) Hardware

The laser's frequency offset from the atomic transition; acts as a reward or cost for excitation.

Rabi oscillation Hardware

The back-and-forth swing of an atom between |g⟩ and |r⟩ under a constant drive.

π-pulse Hardware

A pulse of area π that fully flips |g⟩ to |r⟩ (on resonance). A π/2-pulse makes a 50/50 state.

Van der Waals interaction Hardware

The repulsion C₆/R⁶ between two Rydberg atoms.

C₆ coefficient Hardware

The strength constant of the interaction; 865,723 rad·µm⁶/µs for n = 60.

Rydberg blockade Hardware

Close atoms can't both be excited, because the interaction pushes |rr⟩ out of resonance.

Blockade radius Hardware

Rb = (C₆/Ω)^(1/6): atoms closer than this block each other. About 6.4 µm at full power.

Global addressing Hardware

One laser beam drives every atom identically; the only mode on today's cloud QPUs.

Local addressing Hardware

Driving only selected atoms with a steered beam.

Fluorescence imaging Hardware

Photographing glowing atoms to see which traps are full; dark sites after a sequence read as 1.

SPAM errors Hardware

State preparation and measurement errors: missing atoms, false positives, false negatives.

Dephasing Hardware

Random drift of the phase between qubit levels, which washes out quantum effects.

Orion Hardware

Pasqal's QPU family: Alpha (Fresnel), Beta (100 qubits), Gamma (140–200 qubits).

Repetition rate Hardware

Shots per second; low on neutral atoms (Pasqal Cloud estimates 4 s per shot) because atoms are reloaded every time.

Analog computing Algorithms

Programming by shaping a Hamiltonian's evolution over time instead of applying gates.

Digital computing Algorithms

Gate-based computing: discrete operations on chosen qubits.

Digital-analog (DAQC) Algorithms

Mixing digital gates with analog evolution blocks.

CZ gate Algorithms

Controlled-Z, a two-qubit gate built from the blockade with a π, 2π, π pulse sequence.

Adiabatic theorem Algorithms

A slowly changed system stays in its instantaneous ground state.

QAA Algorithms

Quantum Adiabatic Algorithm: sweep δ slowly from negative to positive so atoms end in the problem's ground state.

Minimum gap Algorithms

The smallest energy difference between ground and first excited state during a sweep; sets how slow the sweep must be.

QAOA Algorithms

Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm: alternating evolutions whose durations are tuned by a classical optimiser.

Variational algorithm Algorithms

A loop in which a classical optimiser tunes quantum parameters using measured results.

Independent set Algorithms

A set of graph nodes with no edge between any two of them.

MIS / MWIS Algorithms

Maximum (weighted) independent set: the largest (or heaviest) independent set. Native to the blockade.

Unit-disk graph Algorithms

Nodes are points; edges join points closer than a fixed distance. Exactly what a register with a blockade radius is.

QUBO Algorithms

Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation: minimise Σ Qᵢⱼ zᵢ zⱼ over binary zᵢ.

Embedding Algorithms

Choosing atom positions (and detunings) so the hardware's energy reproduces a problem.

Antiferromagnetic (AFM) state Algorithms

A checkerboard pattern of excited and ground atoms; the classic analog demo.

Ising model Algorithms

The textbook model of interacting magnetic spins; the Rydberg Hamiltonian is a version of it.

QEK Algorithms

Quantum Evolution Kernel: a graph-similarity measure from QPU fingerprints, used in classical ML.

State-vector emulation Algorithms

Exact simulation storing all 2^N amplitudes; practical to ~25 atoms.

Matrix product state (MPS) Algorithms

An approximate chain-of-tensors representation of a quantum state; cheap when entanglement is low.

Bond dimension Algorithms

The size of the links in an MPS; how much entanglement it can capture.

Pulser Software

Register Software

A named set of atom positions (µm). Its order sets the bit order.

Register layout Software

All trap positions a machine provides; registers choose traps from it. Required on QPUs.

Mappable register Software

A register whose trap assignment is chosen at build time; lets one batch use different registers.

Device Software

The machine's rules: atom count, spacing, channels and their limits.

VirtualDevice / MockDevice Software

Configurable or unconstrained devices for learning and prototyping.

Channel Software

One controllable laser or microwave source, such as rydberg_global.

DMM Software

Detuning map modulator: adds weighted extra detuning to chosen atoms.

SLM mask Software

Detunes chosen atoms far away so a global pulse skips them.

EOM mode Software

A channel mode giving sharper square pulses.

Pulse Software

Amplitude waveform + detuning waveform + phase over one duration.

Waveform Software

A shape over time: Constant, Ramp, Blackman, Kaiser, Interpolated, Composite, Custom.
